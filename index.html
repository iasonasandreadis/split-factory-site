Case study · July to September 2026 · Athens

I built a machine that made, posted and got paid for short videos while I was at school.

It was called Split Factory. It ran on my Mac for eleven weeks, rendered about 850 videos, and posted them across ten accounts. Then the numbers told me to switch it off. This page is the honest record of both halves.

I'm Jason, 17, a high-school student in Athens. I make short videos for software companies, and this was my attempt to automate myself.

Real posts, still live on YouTube. Tap one to play it.

One video, start to finish

0 to 14s, the hook. A found talking clip on top, a found food or cleaning clip below, captions placed on the seam. The machine chose the pair, framed the face, and cut at the end of a sentence.

14s on, the demo. Me, showing the product I was paid to promote. This half is my own footage, filmed once and reused with different hooks.

What it did every day, without me

  1. 01

    Found clips

    Searched YouTube each morning for talking clips and background clips, rejected anything too short, too low quality or watermarked.

  2. 02

    Judged them

    An AI model scored every found clip and took a last look at every finished video. 1,485 calls in September cost $15.95.

  3. 03

    Asked me

    Sent the keepers to a page on my phone. Swipe to keep or skip. Skipping also pulled any unposted video built on that clip.

  4. 04

    Built videos

    Paired clips so no combination repeated, cut on the sentence, added captions, levelled the audio, and nudged colour and speed so no two files matched.

  5. 05

    Posted them

    Booked tomorrow's posts every night at 21:00, spread across the day, with a slow ramp for new accounts and a pause for any account whose views halved.

  6. 06

    Got paid

    Waited four days for views to settle, then submitted each link to the campaign tier that paid that video the most.

  7. 07

    Told me

    A self-test at 06:00, an alarm if nothing had posted by 20:00, a report each night. All to my phone, in plain sentences.

As engineering it worked. As a business it did not. The next section is why.

What I learned by measuring

Finding 1

The machine matched my median. It never made an outlier. The money is all outliers.

Before I built it, I made videos by hand for the same campaigns. I compared the two sets on the platform's own numbers. A typical machine video did almost as well as a typical hand-made one. But my three best hand-made videos earned $862.90, more than twice what all 237 machine posts earned together.

Pay-per-view rewards the one video in fifty that takes off. A machine recombining existing clips produced fifty decent videos and no great one.

Finding 2

YouTube shows about three Shorts per channel per day. The fourth gets nothing.

The machine posted six a day to one channel. I grouped views by the order each video went up that day. Uploads one to three averaged 600 to 1,000 views. Uploads four to six got 0 or 1.

I checked the two obvious excuses. The same hook got 857 views as upload one and 0 as upload four. The same evening time slot got 788 views when it was the day's only upload. Instagram showed the same late videos normally. So it was the count. I capped YouTube at three a day and sent the rest to Instagram only.

Finding 3

The highest-paying campaign is a trap.

One tier paid $3 per 1,000 views with a 400-view minimum. Another paid $4 with a 900-view minimum. A submission cannot be moved afterwards. A video with 500 views earns $1.50 in the first and nothing in the second. So the machine waited for views to settle and routed each video on its own numbers. I learned this after a 5,000-view video got locked into the lower rate.

Finding 4

The worst bugs were the ones that reported success.

For six nights in July the machine rendered every video, failed every upload because the laptop was asleep, and exited as if all was well. 83 videos piled up before I noticed. After that, a run that should have posted and posted nothing became an error, and the machine got a morning self-test and alarms to my phone.

Eleven weeks

  1. 7 Jul

    First batch: 25 videos in a format I had been editing by hand.

  2. 17 Jul

    Measured the YouTube three-a-day limit and capped posting to match.

  3. 21 Jul

    Full bug hunt after a mis-routed payout: 23 bugs fixed, two of them security holes in my own dashboard.

  4. 28 Jul

    Found the six-night silent outage. Added retries and loud failures.

  5. Jul–Aug

    Shared the code with a friend. He adapted it into his own, much larger system.

  6. 28 Aug

    Started version two on top of his core. 231 commits in the next 23 days.

  7. 4 Sep

    School was about to start. Rebuilt it to run alone: automatic clip finding, phone review, alerts.

  8. 19 Sep

    The campaign changed its rules. I paused it, read the numbers above, and did not restart it.

What I did, and what I did not

Mine

  • The format, which I had tested by hand before automating it.
  • Every rule the machine followed, each one from something I measured or something that broke.
  • The product decisions, including a long list of features I said no to.
  • All testing against real accounts, real platforms and real money.
  • The demos in every video, and running the ten accounts.

Not mine

  • I did not type the code. I am not a trained programmer. I built this by directing AI coding agents, Claude Cowork and then Claude Code: describing what I wanted, reading what came back, testing it, and sending it back when it was wrong.
  • The core of version two is a friend's. He adapted my first version into his own system and grew it far beyond mine. I rebuilt on his ledger, campaign routing and dashboard base. The split-screen builder, clip finding, AI judging, posting, phone review and alerts were built on my side.
  • The top and bottom clips in the hooks are other creators' footage. That is how this format works, and it is part of why I stopped.

Version two: about 41,000 lines of Python, a set of small Swift helpers, 12 test files. It ran from 28 August to late September 2026 and is now retired.
