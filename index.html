Case study · July to November 2026 · Athens

We built a machine that makes and posts short videos. In three months it reached 4.7 million views.

It makes short videos for paid UGC campaigns, posts them across a network of accounts, tracks every view, and sends the links to be paid. I started it alone in July. Then I worked on it with a group of friends, coaching them on how to make it better, and we rebuilt it on a far larger scale, posting mostly to Instagram. We are still scaling it.

I'm Jason, 17, a high-school student in Athens. I make short videos for software companies, and this started as my attempt to automate myself.

Where the money comes from

For us: paid UGC campaigns

The system posts for campaigns on MediaMaxxing, a platform where software companies pay creators for views. Campaigns pay from $3 to $10 for every 1,000 views a post gets, usually with a minimum number of views before a post earns anything. The system sends each post's link to the campaign it belongs to, and it has sent 465 so far.

The exact payout total sits in each of our MediaMaxxing accounts. I will add it here once I have the statements.

For the brands: what those views were worth

The CEO of OpenArt, one of the brands we promoted, told us they make roughly $20 to $40 in revenue for every 1,000 views a campaign video gets. Using their own figure on our recorded views:

4,704,346 views ÷ 1,000 × $20 to $40about $94,000 to $188,000

This is an estimate built on one brand's own rule of thumb, not a sales record, and the views were spread across several brands.

The most-viewed post, start to finish

144,326 views. A hook with a caption over it, then the steps shown on screen, then a person talking to camera. The system combines pieces like these into new videos and tracks every reuse.

What the system does

  1. 01

    Builds videos

    Combines footage, openings, tutorials, captions and music into short vertical videos, in several formats.

  2. 02

    Tracks reuse

    Records which pieces went into every video, so no combination repeats and each account gets its own videos.

  3. 03

    Schedules posts

    Posts through scheduling services, with a ramp that starts new accounts slowly and grows them as they warm up.

  4. 04

    Reposts winners

    Brings a video that already worked back onto the same account, under its own rules.

  5. 05

    Watches views

    Saves every post and its view count each day, so performance is measured from records, not memory.

  6. 06

    Sends links to be paid

    Routes each post's link to the right paid campaign, by the account it came from.

What we learned by measuring

Finding 1

Views come from outliers, so the system is built to produce a lot of chances.

The typical post got 1,773 views. The best got 144,326, more than 80 times as many. One account carried most of the reach, and a handful of posts carried that account.

You cannot predict which video will take off. You can make sure there are enough videos, on enough accounts, for the outliers to happen. That is why posting volume and variety matter more than any single video.

Finding 2

Instagram carried almost everything.

Of 4.7 million recorded views, 4.69 million came from Instagram. On my first version YouTube had worked, but it only shows about three Shorts per channel per day. The fourth upload of the day got nothing, whatever the video. So we put the volume on Instagram.

Finding 3

The highest-paying campaign is a trap.

One tier paid $3 per 1,000 views with a 400-view minimum. Another paid $4 with a 900-view minimum. A submission cannot be moved afterwards. A video with 500 views earns $1.50 in the first and nothing in the second. So each link waits for its views to settle and goes to the campaign that pays that video the most.

Finding 4

The worst bugs are the ones that report success.

On my first version, for six nights the machine made every video, failed every upload because the laptop was asleep, and finished as if all was well. 83 videos piled up before I noticed. Since then, a run that should have posted and posted nothing is an error, with a self-test every morning and alarms to my phone.

Where it started

What I did, and what I did not

Mine

  • The first version, Split Factory, and its format, which I had tested by hand before automating it.
  • The rules I measured on my own accounts, like the YouTube limit and the payout routing, which carried into the scaled system.
  • Working on the scaled system with my friends and coaching them on how to make it better.
  • Testing against real accounts, real platforms and real money.

Not mine

  • I did not type the code. I am not a trained programmer. I built my versions by directing AI coding agents, Claude Cowork and then Claude Code: describing what I wanted, reading what came back, testing it, and sending it back when it was wrong.
  • The scaled system is a team effort. Most of its code was written by my friends, on their machines, and the accounts that post its videos are theirs.
  • The clips in many hooks are other creators' footage. That is how this format works.
